What we look for

  • Tea and coffee services — three-, five- and seven-piece sets, American, English and Continental, with or without the tray. Kettles on stands, hot-water urns and chocolate pots too.
  • Trays, salvers and waiters — from a small card tray to a two-handled gallery tray that takes two people to carry.
  • Candelabra and candlesticks — pairs and sets of four especially; convertible candelabra with their original arms.
  • Hollowware — bowls, pitchers and ewers, compotes, covered vegetable dishes, tureens, baskets, wine coolers, cups and trophies.
  • Early and collectible silver — Georgian and Victorian English pieces, American coin silver, Continental 800 and 830 standard, Arts & Crafts and Art Deco makers, and old Sheffield plate of the fused kind.
  • Maker-marked pieces — Tiffany & Co., Gorham (especially Martelé and the special-order lines), Kirk and Stieff repoussé, Georg Jensen, Paul Storr and the London makers, Puiforcat, Buccellati.
  • Silver-mounted things — dresser sets, claret jugs, cane handles, frames, inkwells and desk pieces.

What makes a piece worth more than its metal

With antique silver the metal is the least interesting thing about it. What we are reading when we turn a teapot over:

  • The maker. A clear maker's mark from a sought-after workshop changes everything; an unmarked or retailer-only piece is judged on its quality alone.
  • The period and the form. A Georgian coffee pot, a Victorian naturalistic tea service and a 1930s Jensen bowl appeal to different collectors — and rare forms (a kettle on stand, a monteith, a wine cooler) outrun common ones.
  • The quality of the work. Hand-chasing, cast borders, crisp engraving and good weight in the hand, as opposed to thin, stamped, machine-finished pieces.
  • Condition and honesty. Original surface, no splits, no erased monograms, no later decoration, handles and finials that belong. A little wear is fine; a heavy repair is not.
  • Completeness. The tray that matches the service, the liner that belongs to the tureen, both of a pair.

Reading the marks

Turn the piece over. American silver usually says STERLING or 925 beside the maker's mark. English silver carries a row of small punches — a lion passant for the standard, a town mark, a date letter and the maker's initials — which between them tell you where and in which year it was assayed. Continental pieces read 800, 830 or 835. Early American pieces may read COIN or carry only a silversmith's name — bring those in; some are the best things we see. Our guide to reading silver hallmarks walks through each system.

Plate is a different thing: EPNS, EP, A1, Quadruple plate, or a maker's name with no standard mark. Good, heavy, older plate — a fine Victorian tea service, a handsome gallery tray — is decorative and saleable, and we buy it as what it is. Weighted candlesticks and trumpet vases are thin silver over a filled core; they are priced as objects, by the pair, on their looks.

Before you bring it in

  • Don't polish it. Tarnish comes off; over-polishing takes the crisp detail — and the value — with it.
  • Keep the pieces together — the whole service, the tray, the liners, the fitted case if there is one.
  • Photograph the marks if you'd like an opinion by email: the base of the teapot, the underside of the tray, close enough to read.
  • Bring what you know — a receipt, an old appraisal, the family story of where it came from. Provenance is part of the object.

Buy, consign, or auction?

Most good silver sells best to us, outright. A rare Georgian piece, a Jensen service, a great American maker in original condition, or a piece with a documented history can do better at a specialist sale — and when that's true we'll say so, and place it for you. A fine service you're in no hurry to sell can also go on our floor on consignment. For a whole house of silver, china and furniture, see our estate services.

Questions people ask

Is an antique tea service worth more than its silver?

A good one, usually yes — sometimes many times over. The maker, the period, the quality of the chasing and the condition decide it. A thin, plain, machine-made service is judged more modestly. Bring it in, or send photographs of the whole set and of the marks, and we'll tell you which you have.

It's monogrammed — does that ruin it?

No. A period monogram or crest is part of an antique piece's history and most collectors accept it. What hurts is a monogram that has been erased, leaving a thin, dished patch in the metal.

Do you buy silverplate?

Good, heavy, older plate — a fine Victorian tea service, a gallery tray, old Sheffield — yes, as the decorative object it is. Everyday modern plate has very little resale value, and we'll tell you so rather than waste your trip.

What about my sterling flatware?

Flatware sets, and any silver that is really being sold for its weight, are handled by our jewelry branch, Naples Estate Jewelry. If the tea service and the flatware chest are both coming out of the same sideboard, one visit covers both.

Will you come to the house for a large collection?

Yes, anywhere from Marco Island to Fort Myers, by appointment. For a whole house of silver, china and furniture, that's our estate service.

Jewelry, gold and coins, or watches in the mix? Those go to our jewelry branch, Naples Estate Jewelry — same address, same owner.
