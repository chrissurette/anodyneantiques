How to tell if silver is sterling.

Turn it over. Nine times in ten the answer is stamped on the bottom, and the tenth time there are tells. Here's how to read what you're holding — and why the answer changes the value by a factor of ten or more.

By Chris, Anodyne Antiques · Updated September 2026 · 6 min read

What "sterling" means

Sterling is an alloy of 92.5% silver and 7.5% other metal (usually copper), solid all the way through. Silverplate is a base metal — copper, brass, nickel silver — with a thin layer of silver bonded to the outside. They can look identical on a sideboard. One is worth its weight in silver and often much more as an object; the other is worth what someone will pay for the object alone, which for most plated flatware is very little.

Step one: read the marks

Look on the underside of hollowware, the back of a fork or spoon handle, the rim of a tray. You'll usually need a magnifying glass and good light. Marks that mean sterling:

  • STERLING or STER — the standard American mark since the late 1800s, often with a maker's name or symbol.
  • 925, .925 or 925/1000 — the same standard, used by American, Mexican and modern makers worldwide.
  • A lion passant (a lion walking, facing left) — the English sterling standard mark, usually alongside a town mark, a date letter and a maker's mark. Reading a full set of English hallmarks.
  • COIN, PURE COIN, DOLLAR or STANDARD — American "coin silver," roughly 90% silver, made before sterling became the norm. Older, often more valuable. Many early coin pieces carry only the maker's name.
  • 800, 830, 835, 900, 950 — Continental European standards in parts per thousand. Not technically "sterling" but solid silver, bought as such.

Marks that mean plate:

  • EPNS (electroplated nickel silver), EP, EPBM, EPC.
  • Silverplate, Silver plated, Plate, A1, Quadruple plate, Triple plate.
  • Sheffield on anything made after about 1850 — modern "Sheffield" is a plating trade name, not the old fused Sheffield plate, which is unmarked and collected in its own right.
  • A maker's name with no standard mark at all — "Wm. Rogers," "Oneida," "Community," "International Silver Co." on their own almost always mean plate. Confusingly, some of those makers also made sterling, which they marked STERLING. If it doesn't say so, assume it isn't.
  • "Nickel silver" or "German silver" — contains no silver whatsoever.

Step two: the tells, when there's no mark

  • Wear. Plate wears through at the high points — the bowl of a spoon, the edge of a tray, the tips of fork tines — showing yellowish brass or grey base metal. Sterling wears, but stays silver-colored all the way down.
  • Weight and sound. Sterling hollowware is often lighter than the same shape in plated brass, and rings with a clearer, longer note when tapped. Neither test is proof.
  • Tarnish color. Sterling tarnishes to a warm grey-black; plate can tarnish blotchy, or show green-blue copper corrosion at worn spots.
  • The magnet. Silver isn't magnetic, but neither are brass, copper or nickel silver — so a magnet only rules out steel. It never confirms sterling.
  • Seams and construction. Hand-raised sterling hollowware and cast handles look and feel different from stamped, seamed plated pieces. This one takes experience.

Tests to skip

Acid test kits will scar a piece and can't tell sterling from heavy plate at a scratch point. Don't file a notch in anything. Don't put ice on it. If the marks and tells leave you unsure, photograph the piece and its marks and ask someone who handles silver daily — we'll tell you for free, and we'll tell you if it's plate. Free evaluations.

Weighted sterling — the in-between case

Candlesticks, trumpet vases, compotes and trophies are often marked STERLING WEIGHTED or WEIGHTED. They're sterling on the outside with a thin skin over cement or pitch. The metal value is small; they're priced as decorative objects, and a good pair still sells. Just don't expect them to weigh in like a tea service.

Why it matters this much

A twelve-place sterling flatware service in a good pattern can be worth several thousand dollars; the same pattern in plate is worth a fraction. A sterling tea service is bought as an object with a floor set by its metal; a plated one is bought only as an object, and usually a modest one. Knowing which you have is the difference between a line on the estate inventory and a line in the donation box — and between a fair offer and a bad one. How we buy sterling, and how we price it.

One more time: don't polish it

Tarnish is not damage. Aggressive polishing rounds off the crisp detail that makes old silver worth more, and it can take the plate right off a plated piece and reveal the answer the hard way. Leave it as it is; we'll tell you what you have.
